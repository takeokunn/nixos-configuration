# Prose mechanics, paragraphs, and argument

Applies to any human-facing prose: articles, reports, PR/issue bodies, and documentation. The rules are
language-independent and govern drafting.

## Passages and instructions

Classify each passage before writing it, and do not mix the two in one passage:

- **Procedural**: tells the reader what to do; imperative mood, one instruction per sentence.
- **Descriptive**: explains what a thing is or does; no imperative, one new point per sentence.

Put the condition before the command ("If the build fails, read the log"; a trailing condition gets dropped
by a skimming reader). Describe an action with a verb, not a nominalization ("compress the file", not
"perform compression"). A requirement is "must"; a capability is "can"; do not write "should" in
instructions: readers treat it as optional.

## Paragraphs

- Use paragraph writing: a paragraph is one step of argument, and the reader must be able to follow the logic
  paragraph by paragraph.
- The first sentence of a paragraph reveals what the paragraph is about.
- Make the relation between paragraphs clear; add a connective only when the relation would otherwise be
  ambiguous.
- Lead with the conclusion when it helps the reader, then give grounds and address objections without
  repeating the conclusion mechanically.
- Defenses of an example (it looks contrived, pre-empting a doubt) must not break the flow mid-climax; handle
  them together at the start of the next section.
- Explicitly deny a likely wrong reading before stating the real reason ("The reason is not X. It is Y.").
- In a concession ("Admittedly, ..."), stay at confirming the fact. Do not assert, in the author's voice or as
  causation, something you later correct. To grant a surface diagnosis provisionally, attribute it to the
  reader or a common view ("this could be summarized as ...").
- Do not reveal climax information (figures, specific facts) in the paragraph before the climax.
- When negating or limiting, quote the exact proposition being negated ("this does not mean that a written
  rule lets you delegate everything"); do not settle for a vague negation such as "not everything is solved".

## Argument

- Do not lump distinct things together as "the same". Three interdependent, undecided decisions are not "the
  same decision made separately"; write that each is a separate decision and that they depend on one another.
- Do not reduce a multi-factor event to a single cause. If an example contains several kinds of problems,
  separate them and map which concept explains which.
- Keep the treatment of a concept consistent across chapters and sections: do not classify a decision as made
  by one person in one section and by team agreement in another. Classification, definition, and term status
  stay uniform throughout.
- A point deferred to a later section must be covered there; do not plant foreshadowing you never collect.
- After a concession or limitation ("however", "that said"), advance the argument; do not end on the
  adversative and leave it hanging.
- State a section's central term's definition or scope before it is used, within or before that section.
- When merging several concepts under one umbrella term, state in one sentence just before naming it that
  they reduce to the same thing; also bridge the reverse (decomposition) operation.

## Reader load

- Omit decorative names, but preserve file names, identifiers, and other locators needed to verify a claim,
  even when they appear only once.
- When an abstract phrase's referent is not fixed by context, pin it down in place with a parenthetical
  apposition so the reader need not look back.
- When a new example or scene adds context the reader must hold, preface it with what differs from the prior
  example and why another is needed.
- In chapter and section introductions, do not pack in detail unrelated to the example that follows.

## Redundancy

- Combine parallel facts with the same logical role into one sentence, and mark their logical status with
  the lead word ("Naturally, both the monthly close and customer payments ...").
- If a multi-sentence argument compresses to one sentence, keep only that sentence.
- Do not use an imagined reader Q&A as rhetoric (posing a question and answering it in one word); state the
  claim directly. Do not act out the reader's reaction ("You may have felt X. That is right."); make
  concessions plainly in the body.
- Do not frame a likely reader idea with meta scaffolding ("there is a natural continuation of this story");
  write the idea directly. A reader's question may stay as a question.
- Do not write author-stance disclaimers ("this book does not deny that either"); state only the fact.
- Share context with the reader in the fewest steps; if the point lands without unrolling every step, name
  the structure and assert it.
- Do not bring in concepts or document names the body has not introduced yet.

## Headings

- Make a heading specific enough to identify the section: the question it answers or the object it treats. A
  noun phrase naming that object is enough.
- Do not use procedure-only headings ("Back to the example", "Rereading X") or headings that carry no
  information.
- Keep the conclusion, a warning, or a punchline out of the heading; the body states it.

## Honesty to the reader

- If an example may look contrived, do not hide it; pre-empt the reader's doubt and add brief grounds that it
  is realistically plausible.
- Ground plausibility in a cited observation or explain the example's assumptions. Do not replace an
  unsupported assertion with an invented appeal to common experience.
- Do not write unconfirmed things as if confirmed.
